How to write a scholarship CV for international applications

A scholarship CV is a selective evidence document, not a complete autobiography. Its job is to help a reviewer find your academic preparation, relevant experience, research potential, leadership and contribution quickly enough to understand why you fit the award.

Updated 21 Oct 2026Official source record ↓

The short answer

A scholarship CV presents relevant education, projects, research, achievements and experience in an easy-to-scan format. Start with the programme’s instructions: not every application requests a separate CV or accepts the same length.

Next step: Open the practical CV skeleton below. Keep only sections supported by your own record and tailor the order to the selection criteria.

Choose the example that fits your stage

A CV with no employment history can still show academic projects, volunteering or relevant independent work. A research applicant needs different evidence from a first-degree applicant. Do not invent publications or use a scholarship logo to make a document look official.

Write achievement bullets instead of duty lists

A useful bullet combines an action, context and result. Replace responsible for organising events with evidence such as coordinated a five-member team to deliver three career workshops for 180 students, collecting feedback that informed the next semester's programme. Use numbers only when they are true and meaningful.

Not every result needs a percentage. Research quality, a report adopted by a department, a process improved, a participant group reached or a difficult problem solved can all be specific outcomes. Begin with a strong verb, make your contribution clear and remove unnecessary introductory phrases.

A practical scholarship CV skeleton

Use this as a planning outline, not a fixed template. Remove sections that contain no useful evidence and reorder the remainder for the award's current criteria.

  • FULL NAME — professional email | phone with country code | location | relevant profile link
  • ACADEMIC PROFILE — current discipline, strongest relevant evidence and future direction
  • EDUCATION — degree, institution, dates, original grade, thesis and selected distinction
  • RESEARCH OR PROJECTS — objective, contribution, methods and outcome
  • EXPERIENCE — role, organisation, dates and evidence-based bullets
  • LEADERSHIP AND SERVICE — action, people or problem involved and result
  • PUBLICATIONS OR PRESENTATIONS — complete citation and honest status
  • AWARDS, SKILLS AND LANGUAGES — selected, specific and supportable information

An illustrative before-and-after CV entry

Weak: Worked on a water-quality research project and helped with data analysis. Stronger: Analysed 240 water samples from six collection sites using the team's approved protocol, identified two recurring contamination patterns and prepared figures used in the department's final report.

The stronger version gives scope, contribution and output without inventing impact. Build each bullet from your own records. Scholarship committees may verify claims through transcripts, supervisors, references or interviews.

Scholarship CV, academic CV and job resume

The document name does not always tell you the required format. A scholarship CV often emphasises education, academic projects, research, awards, leadership and service. A job resume usually gives more space to employment achievements and skills matched to a vacancy. A full academic CV can extend further to publications, conferences, teaching, grants and professional activity.

Read the application instructions before choosing a template. If a scholarship supplies its own form or CV template, use it exactly. The Rhodes Scholarship, for example, can provide constituency-specific CV instructions, while a postgraduate course may accept a conventional academic CV. General advice should never override the current application requirement.

How long should a scholarship CV be?

There is no universal page limit. Some scholarship processes request one or two pages, a supplied template may set a fixed limit and a research-focused academic application may allow a longer document. Oxford's academic application guidance advises applicants to remain concise and relevant rather than assuming one standard length.

When no limit is stated, early-stage applicants can usually present the strongest evidence within one or two focused pages. Do not remove important research or publications merely to follow an invented rule, but do not add weak activities to make the CV look longer. Relevance and scanability matter more than page count.

Choose the section order from the selection criteria

The best order depends on what the award evaluates and where your strongest proof sits. Education and research may lead for an academic master's or PhD scholarship. Leadership and community impact may move higher for an award that explicitly selects on those qualities. A professional fellowship may place relevant work before older academic detail.

Use reverse chronological order within most sections unless a required template says otherwise. Keep headings conventional so a reviewer can find information quickly.

  • Name and professional contact details
  • Optional academic profile
  • Education and academic distinctions
  • Research, thesis and selected projects
  • Relevant employment and internships
  • Leadership, volunteering and service
  • Publications, presentations and awards where relevant
  • Technical skills, languages and professional memberships

Write a clean header and useful academic profile

Start with your full name, a professional email address, phone number with country code and current country or city when useful. Add LinkedIn, ORCID, Google Scholar, a portfolio or another professional link only when it is accurate, current and worth the reviewer's time. A complete street address is rarely necessary unless requested.

An academic profile is optional. If used, keep it to two or three lines describing your current level, field, strongest relevant capability and intended direction. Avoid generic objectives such as seeking a challenging opportunity or claiming to be passionate and hardworking without evidence.

Present education so an international reader can understand it

List the official degree title, institution, location and completion or expected completion date. Include the grade, CGPA or classification when relevant and accurate. Do not convert a score to another scale unless the programme instructs you to do so or an authorised evaluation provides the conversion.

Add a thesis, dissertation, selected modules or academic distinction only when it supports the application. If the local grading system may be unfamiliar, provide a brief accurate note based on the transcript or institution's official scale instead of describing your result as equivalent to a foreign grade on your own authority.

  • Official degree and institution name
  • Dates and expected graduation where applicable
  • Original grade or CGPA scale
  • Thesis or final project title
  • Selected honours or relevant coursework

Turn research and academic projects into evidence

Research experience should show more than a project title. State the question or objective, your contribution, the methods or tools used and the output or learning. Distinguish your individual work from the work of a team or supervisor.

Coursework can be valuable when formal experience is limited. Select projects that demonstrate the skills the programme needs, such as analysis, fieldwork, laboratory technique, design, coding, writing or community research. Describe one strong project clearly rather than listing every assignment.

  • Research question or project objective
  • Your individual contribution
  • Method, data, tools or population
  • Result, output or presentation
  • Capability relevant to the proposed programme

Present work, internships, leadership and service selectively

Employment is relevant when it demonstrates preparation, judgement or impact connected to the award. Include the organisation, role, dates and two to four focused bullets. Do not copy a job description. Translate the experience into evidence the scholarship committee can evaluate.

Leadership is not limited to holding a president title. Mentoring, coordinating volunteers, improving a process, representing students or initiating a community response can show leadership when your decisions and results are clear. List membership without explanation only when it is genuinely significant.

List publications and presentations honestly

Use one consistent citation style and label the status accurately. Published, accepted, in press, submitted and in preparation are not interchangeable. Do not place an unfinished idea under publications, and do not imply first authorship or sole responsibility when the work was collaborative.

Separate peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations, posters, reports or public writing when the distinctions matter. For a short scholarship CV, select the most relevant items and provide a complete professional profile link only if the programme allows it.

Build a strong CV without formal work experience

Students are not expected to invent employment. Use relevant academic projects, thesis work, laboratory or field experience, tutoring, volunteering, student societies, competitions, independent learning and family or community responsibilities that demonstrate real skills.

The standard remains evidence. Explain what you produced, improved, analysed, organised or learned. A well-described final-year project can be more persuasive than an unrelated part-time role included only to fill space.

  • Thesis and substantial coursework
  • Research assistance or laboratory work
  • Volunteering and community initiatives
  • Student leadership and event delivery
  • Competitions, portfolios and verified certifications
  • Relevant independent or collaborative projects

Tailor the CV without rewriting your history

Tailoring means selecting and ordering accurate evidence for the reader. Read the scholarship criteria and mark the experiences that prove academic readiness, leadership, service, research potential or professional impact. Move the most relevant evidence higher and use terminology that is natural for the field.

Maintain a master CV containing your complete verified history, then create a focused version for each application. Do not change dates, inflate titles or add keywords you cannot explain. The application form, CV, essays and references should agree.

Use Europass only when it serves the application

Europass is an official European tool that can create CVs for employment, education, training and volunteering. It is useful when a programme requests or accepts that format, or when you need a consistent starting structure. It is not automatically the best format for every scholarship.

If no template is required, compare the Europass output with the selection criteria and available space. A simpler custom document may present academic projects or research more efficiently. Whichever format you choose, tailor the content rather than submitting a complete profile containing every activity.

Keep the design quiet and readable

Use a readable typeface, consistent dates, clear headings, adequate white space and restrained emphasis. Avoid rating bars, excessive colours, icons that replace important labels and multi-column layouts that make the reading order unclear. A scholarship CV should look organised rather than decorative.

Submit PDF unless the portal requests another format. Open the final file on a phone and computer, confirm that links work, check that no line has been cut off and use a professional file name such as Firstname-Lastname-Scholarship-CV.pdf. Do not include a photograph, age, identity number, marital status or other sensitive information unless the current instructions genuinely require it.

Additional CV checks for Pakistani applicants

Use the exact English degree and institution names shown on official records where available. Keep Pakistani grades on their original scale unless the programme requests a conversion, and explain expected graduation, result awaiting or degree completion accurately.

Local society work, volunteering, teaching, internships and community responsibilities can be strong evidence when described with context and results. Avoid unexplained abbreviations that an international reviewer may not recognise. If a certification, award or organisation is important, give enough information to show what it represents without turning the CV into an essay.

Common scholarship CV mistakes

Most weak CVs are difficult to evaluate rather than visually unattractive. The reviewer cannot quickly identify relevance, contribution or progression. Remove anything that creates uncertainty without adding useful evidence.

  • Using one generic job resume for every scholarship
  • Writing an unsupported objective or long personal biography
  • Listing duties, memberships and skills without evidence
  • Converting grades or exaggerating publication status
  • Including every activity regardless of relevance
  • Using inconsistent dates, titles or formatting
  • Adding decorative skill bars, graphics or excessive colours
  • Ignoring a required template or page limit
  • Submitting a file that is unreadable or incorrectly named

Final scholarship CV checklist

Review the CV against the actual selection criteria, then ask whether a reader can locate your strongest evidence within a short scan. Complete a factual review before correcting language and layout.

  • The required template, length and file type are followed
  • Contact details and professional links are current
  • The strongest relevant section appears early
  • Degrees, grades, dates and role titles are accurate
  • Bullets show contribution and outcome
  • Publication and project status is honest
  • Unfamiliar abbreviations and grading information are clear
  • The CV matches the form, essays and references
  • Spelling, alignment, page breaks and file name are correct

Frequently asked questions

How long should a CV for a scholarship application be?+

Follow the scholarship's stated limit or template. When no rule is given, many early-stage applicants can present their strongest evidence in one or two pages, while a research-focused academic CV may need more space. Keep every section concise and relevant.

What is the difference between a scholarship CV and a job resume?+

A scholarship CV usually places greater emphasis on education, research, projects, academic achievements, leadership and service. A job resume normally prioritises experience and skills matched to an employer. The scholarship's selection criteria should decide the final emphasis.

Can I make a scholarship CV without work experience?+

Yes. Use substantial coursework, thesis research, academic projects, volunteering, student leadership, tutoring, competitions and relevant independent work. Describe your contribution, method and result rather than inventing employment or filling space with unsupported skills.

Should I use a Europass CV for scholarships?+

Use Europass when the programme requests it or when its structure suits the application. It is not universally required. If no format is specified, choose the clearest document for presenting evidence and tailor it to the scholarship criteria.

Should a scholarship CV include a photo?+

Do not add a photo automatically. Include one only when the current application instructions or accepted local format clearly require it. Avoid unnecessary personal details that do not help the committee assess your eligibility or merit.

Should I include references on my scholarship CV?+

Include referee details only if the programme requests them on the CV. Most applications collect references in a separate section or portal. Do not use valuable space for references available on request unless instructed.
